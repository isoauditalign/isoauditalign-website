If a tender, head contractor, or client has asked you for ISO 45001 and you're starting from scratch, here's what it actually involves — not the marketing version, the practical one.

## What ISO 45001 actually is

ISO 45001 is the international standard for occupational health and safety (OHS/WHS) management systems. It replaced AS/NZS 4801 as the standard Australian certification bodies audit against, and it's built around the same logic as ISO 27001 or ISO 9001: a management system with defined roles, risk assessment, documented controls, and a cycle of audit and improvement — just applied to workplace safety instead of information security or quality.

It is not a safety checklist or a single policy document. It's a system for how your organisation identifies hazards, assesses risk, controls it, and keeps improving — with evidence a certification auditor can test.

## Who actually needs it

- **Construction, trades, and infrastructure** businesses, where it's frequently a prequalification requirement before you can even bid on a tender.
- **Manufacturing and logistics**, where physical hazards (plant, vehicles, manual handling) are a core part of daily operations.
- **Any business in a government or head-contractor supply chain**, where ISO 45001 is increasingly requested as a condition of being an approved supplier — sometimes even for roles that look low-risk on paper.
- Businesses that have had a safety incident, insurance review, or client due diligence process raise WHS governance as a gap.

## What the certification process actually involves

1. **Discovery and scoping** — defining which sites, teams, and activities sit inside the management system, and understanding your actual hazard profile.
2. **Gap analysis** — a clause-by-clause review against what you already have: hazard registers, safe work procedures, incident records, training logs.
3. **Framework implementation** — building out hazard identification and risk assessment processes, the hierarchy of controls, worker consultation mechanisms, and emergency preparedness plans, based on how your sites actually operate, not a generic template.
4. **Internal audit** — a full simulated audit before the real one, to catch and close non-conformances while they're still cheap to fix.
5. **Certification audit (Stage 1 and Stage 2)** — your certification body tests whether the system you've documented actually operates in practice, including site visits where relevant.

## What it costs

As with any ISO certification, cost splits into two buckets: **implementation/consulting** (building the management system) and **certification body audit fees** (paid separately to an accredited certifier like DNV, BSI, or SAI Global). The biggest cost driver isn't headcount on its own — it's how hazardous the actual work is and how many sites are in scope. A single-site professional services business with low physical risk costs meaningfully less to certify than a multi-site operation running plant, vehicles, and subcontractors.

## What happens after certification

Like every ISO standard, ISO 45001 runs on a three-year cycle: annual surveillance audits in years one and two, then a recertification audit in year three. The system needs to keep operating in practice — hazard registers updated, incidents investigated, worker consultation genuinely happening — not just exist as a folder of documents from the original certification project.
